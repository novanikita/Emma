216 Nielsen Norman Group — experienced agent users build their own three-layer "context library" research, opinion Nielsen Norman Group (Tanner Kohler)
2026-09-18
analyst		 NN/g conducted a qualitative study with advanced Claude users who work with agents daily. It turned out that they manually assemble "context libraries" of three types. Global context: rarely changing rules and preferences that apply across all tasks. Local context: files, lists and data for a specific task. Background context: raw streams such as email, meeting transcripts, Slack and analytics. Nobody sorts through them, but the agent must have access to them. But the "global / local / background" split transfers almost one-to-one to memory of a conversation partner: stable traits and agreements, the current topic of conversation, and things said in passing. For Emma: together with PAIR (#2), this is the second signal this week that user-managed memory is valued above "magic" memory. 214 The Batch — "Even agents benefit from reminders": a separate memory agent decides when to remind and when to stay silent research, opinion Meta AI (Yifan Wu, Lizhu Zhang, Yuhang Zhou et al.); analysis by The Batch (DeepLearning.AI, Andrew Ng)
2026-09-18
analyst		 The Batch (issue 371) reviewed the Meta AI paper "Remember When It Matters: Proactive Memory Agent for Long-Horizon Agents" (arXiv 2607.08716, July 2026). The authors call the problem "behavioral state decay": over long tasks, requirements, facts about the environment, past attempts and open subtasks get buried in the context and stop influencing decisions. The solution is a separate memory agent running alongside an unmodified main agent. At every step it updates structured notes (facts, environment, successful fixes, unfinished parts of the task, failed commands) and decides whether to add a short reminder or stay silent. Results: on Terminal-Bench 2.0, Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored 45.9% versus 37.6% without the memory agent; on τ²-Bench (airline, retail, telecom), 61.8% versus 55.0%; a small open version on Qwen3.5-27B after SFT and GRPO scored 41.1% versus 37.6%. The main takeaway The Batch puts in "We're thinking": a system with selective reminders beat one that reminds at every step. "Reminding an agent is not enough. You need to remind it at the right moment." It caught attention because this is exactly one of Emma's stated functions — "recall context" — and the study shows the value lies in choosing the moment, not in the completeness of memory. Layer 7 (The Batch). Primary source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08716. The study is about text agents in the terminal and customer-service settings, not about voice or conversation with a person — the transfer to Emma is hypothetical. Ablations in the paper: selective intervention beats passive access to a memory bank, constant injection, an "advisor-only" mode and plain retrieval. For Emma: (1) the architectural pattern of "a separate listener-observer next to the conversation partner that decides when to intervene" fits the "what Emma notices" block; (2) the principle "silence is also a decision" is worth carrying over to Emma's turns: a reminder or a highlighted contradiction is valuable at the right moment and harmful when delivered constantly; (3) a pair to Adaptive Interruption Handling (batch 4) and "Take the Floor When Asked, Not When Needed" (batch 16) — in each case the question is not "what to say" but "when."

212 PAIR — an emotion-regulation companion lived with 19 people for two weeks: it confused "venting" with "I'm angry" and forgot corrections research Kexin Quan, Zijian Ding, Jiaye Yong, Qinshi Zhang, Dong Wang, Jessie Chin (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, University of Maryland, Florida State University)
2026-09-17
science		 The authors tested PAIR, a companion for emotion regulation, on 19 participants over 14 days: 1,093 sessions, by voice or text. From a person's account, the model estimates emotion on three axes (valence, arousal, dominance) based on cognitive appraisal theory, chooses a regulation strategy and leads the conversation. After each evening session, a lightweight model compresses the day into a memory block of up to 150 words: recurring worries, mismatches between the model's estimates and self-report, user corrections and which strategies worked. This block is fed into the following sessions. Valence matched self-report fairly well (r = 0.669), dominance moderately (r = 0.538), and arousal was barely detected (r = 0.236, κ = 0.154). After low-valence sessions, mood rose by 1.34 points on average, and the perceived helpfulness of the conversation grew significantly over time. Failures: 8 of 19 participants complained that the system "inflated or flattened" the intensity of an emotion ("I just want to vent. I'm not actually angry"). The system forgot corrections, repeated rejected advice and sometimes simply restated the named emotion. The authors' main conclusion: memory should be made visible and editable; users want to see and correct what has been remembered about them. It drew attention as a rare field study of a companion with memory and emotions in real life rather than on a benchmark. Academic paper, preprint (arXiv), USA, English, demo. Only 20.2% of sessions were by voice, and speech was transcribed without prosody. Hence the main weakness: from text you cannot tell how strongly a person feels. This directly echoes VoiceLongMemEval (batch 13): an ASR cascade loses affective signals. Ready-made patterns for Emma: (1) distinguish "I want to vent" from "I want advice," that is, switch between listening and suggesting; (2) remember not only facts but also "what we already tried that didn't work"; (3) store user corrections as the most valuable type of memory. Corrections occurred in only 4% of sessions, but forgetting them was the most irritating.
211 "Full-Duplex Speech Models Take the Floor When Asked, Not When Needed" — voice models almost never step in to challenge a falsehood or warn of danger research Linkai Peng, Baorian Nuchged, Kaiqi Fu, Yuyang Yao (University of Connecticut, The University of Texas at Austin, X Square Robot, University of Oxford)
2026-09-17
science		 People take the floor not only when addressed or when the other person falls silent. We step in on our own to correct a false statement, supply a forgotten word or warn of danger. The authors tested whether full-duplex models do the same. They assembled English monologues in which, within a single topic, only the trigger phrase changes: a direct question, a false fact (for example, a false claim citing an authority), a dangerous action and others, 10 conditions in total based on turn-allocation rules. Pauses between words were compressed so that silence would not provide an extra cue to speak. Five families were tested: Moshi, PersonaPlex (base and RL versions), NVIDIA VoiceChat-11B, MiniCPM-o 4.5 and Raon-SpeechChat. Result: the models reliably respond to being addressed (+0.08…+0.24 over the base rate of starting to speak) and to silence (0.12–0.83), but barely at all to false facts and danger (no more than +0.06). Even when a model does take the floor, among non-empty responses to a false fact only 14–15% challenge the claim, and 4–7% warn about danger. Pauses and explicit permission to interrupt do not close this gap. It drew attention because it tests exactly what is declared as Emma's role: "highlight a contradiction". It turns out today's full-duplex models can step in on turn-taking cues, but not on meaning. Academic work, a preprint (arXiv, 5 pages), English, a demo on open models. Closed models (GPT-Live, Gemini Live) were not tested. A related work is CAPA (Khan et al., "Speak for Me", EMNLP 2026 Main, https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03923): an LLM delegate at a meeting with a single prompt stays silent in 51.4% of the moments where the absent participant would have spoken. An explicit meeting state (positions, what has been covered, who has the floor) cuts the silence to 2.5% with 0.6% hallucinations. For Emma: the decision to "step in" cannot be left to a turn-taking model. It needs a separate semantic layer that tracks the other person's claims and contradictions with what they said earlier, plus an explicit conversation state rather than just a long context.

204 RoleBreak — in a long voice dialogue the first loss of role comes on average at turn 10.4, the first safety failure at turn 11.6 research Yuqi Wang, Fengyuan Liu, Haochen Luo, Zhiqi Yu, Qi Liu
2026-09-15
science		 The benchmark tests whether speech models hold an assigned role over the long run — not only fictional characters but also user-oriented roles (assistant, conversation partner). The set has 310 roles, 6,688 human-verified turns, 11,743 fine-grained criteria and 1,856 turns with a target emotion in the voice. Nine configurations were tested: full-duplex, omni models and ASR→LLM→TTS cascades. Four findings. (1) Systems hold a role far better in meaning than in vocal emotion. (2) Semantic consistency is fragile: even the best system first breaks role after 10.4 turns on average, and the first safety failure comes after 11.6. (3) A larger LLM delays the failure but barely improves vocal emotion. (4) Emotion in the user's voice changes the model's in-role behavior even with identical text. 130 SIGDIAL 2026 — "Rethinking Binary Evaluation of Turn-Taking": a corpus records one of several possible outcomes, not the single correct turn shift research Yunosuke Kubo, Kenta Yamamoto, Ryu Takeda, Kazunori Komatani (Osaka University)
2026-08-02
science		 Turn-taking prediction models output a probability of a speaker change. But they are usually evaluated like this: the probability is thresholded into "yes/no" and compared with what the corpus recorded. The authors point out that this silently treats the recorded turn shift as ground truth.
